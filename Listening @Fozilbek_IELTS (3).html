PART 4 — Whale Shark Conservation Field Trip

Hi everyone. I've just returned from my field trip and want to share some of my experiences with you. I learnt a lot about whale sharks on this trip. For example, they're actually the largest species of fish in the world's seas, about the size of a bus. Despite their massive size, whale sharks are docile animals. Because of the name, you might be confused about whether these animals are whales or sharks. They are in fact sharks, but they do have some things in common with whales. They're similar in terms of their large size and what they eat. Like many species of whales, their diet consists mainly of plankton and krill, which are tiny plants and animals. Unlike many whale species, they prefer to swim close to the surface of the water rather than cruising the depths of the ocean. So that's a key difference relating to their habits. Another one relates to their physical makeup. Their skeleton is made entirely of cartilage, in comparison to whales that have skeletons made of real bones.

The place I visited is called Ningaloo in Western Australia. We were there to observe a large group of whale sharks which congregate in that part of the ocean each year between April and July. Ningaloo is ideal for whale sharks because there is a coral reef nearby which ensures an abundant supply of food. It's great for divers too because although it's in relatively deep waters, the reef is in close proximity to the shore. Unlike some reefs that you have to travel miles to get to.

I'd like to move on now to tell you about the Whale Shark Research Centre and the research being conducted there. We know that whale sharks are migratory, but not a lot is known about where they go and why. The methods currently being used to monitor them could be better. One way is to use an electronic tracking device, or tag, which researchers have to attach manually to the fin. It's not an easy process, and the tags are also quite expensive, so if it falls off, you're in trouble.

Another way of monitoring whale sharks is with a special drone to spot the animals in the ocean. This can give you a rough idea of numbers, but obviously it's not an exact science. The researchers at Ningaloo are involved in a new research project that's currently being carried out to investigate whale shark feeding habits. They hope to find out more about how far whale sharks travel to feed. They're using an innovative approach to do this, which analyses plankton. Apparently, plankton from different places contains different quantities of carbon types. The researchers are checking the plankton consumed by the whale shark against maps which have all the different plankton zones marked on them. This comparison allows them to track exactly where the whale shark has been feeding.

Some research that I was involved in on my field trip was directly observing the whale shark behaviour. We drifted along in our boat with the engine off, then when a whale shark was nearby, we switched on the engine. We noticed that the resulting noise affected the behaviour of the animal. As soon as it heard the sound, we saw it dive. This happened on several occasions and was quite obvious. One day, we got to witness the great work they're doing at the centre to educate the community about whale sharks. The researchers select a mature whale shark which has been tagged and they allocate it to a school. The children get to track the journey of their whale shark and see which one travels the furthest.

It's clear that these majestic creatures require careful conservation and management. While these events may be rare, all injuries which whale sharks suffer must be documented, with information such as date and location and a photograph if possible. The Fisheries Department has agreed to keep these records in their digital image library. Something that is being introduced next year in Ningaloo is a code of conduct. It covers the rules that need to be followed to minimise any distress to the sharks. Scuba diving is not going to be allowed anymore, as previous research has found that whale sharks find bubbles disturbing.

SECTION 4

Good morning. Today I'll be talking about the language Bislama, a form of Pidgin English used in the South Pacific nation of Vanuatu.
Vanuatu is a group of islands where 81 first languages are still regularly used. There are a further 17 that are in danger of dying out, and 8 that have been identified as extinct. [Q31 → extinct] That gives a total of 106 first languages.
As for foreign languages, English has official status, largely because it is the medium for all education purposes. [Q32 → education]
But it is Bislama that is the most widely spoken language, used by more than 90% of the population. In earlier times some people had negative feelings towards it – for many years it was commonly referred to as a broken language. [Q33 → broken] But attitudes have changed.
The first form of Pidgin English can be traced to around 1800, when foreign traders arrived and local people were recruited as sailors.
Then from about 1860, a lot of people from Vanuatu travelled to Australia to work on the new plantations as labourers. [Q34 → plantation]
Finally, from about the 1950s, Vanuatu experienced a significant migration of its people from small villages into the city. [Q35 → city]

How can we describe the language itself? Linguists prefer to describe Bislama as a developed pidgin, because it has more rules and ideas than most simple pidgins. [Q36 → developed]
The majority of Bislama's vocabulary is derived from English. However, the word “from” can also mean “because of”, so words can have a wider range of meanings in Bislama. [Q37 → meanings]
There are some words, around 5–10%, that are derived from French. [Q38 → French]
A small number of words come from local, Pacific languages – usually where there is no English equivalent for natural phenomena, or to describe some aspect of the culture that is unique to the country. [Q39 → culture]
Finally, grammar. The word “long” – it is almost the only preposition in Bislama and may be used in place of at, to, with, on, in, and so on. [Q40 → preposition]

SECTION 4

Today I'd like to talk about the history of the watermelon and how this fruit has changed over time. To begin with, let's look at the earliest ancestors of the watermelon. Wild varieties of this plant once grew widely across the African continent, particularly in the hot and dry deserts of the region. [Q31 → deserts] However, these early fruits were very different from the sweet watermelons we eat today. Their taste was actually quite unpleasant and the flesh was extremely bitter. [Q32 → bitter]

Another interesting feature is that some early types of watermelon had a rough outer surface. In fact, there were varieties that had fine hair, growing on the outside of the fruit. [Q33 → hair] In addition, the inside of these ancient fruits was very hard. The flesh was firm and green rather than soft and juicy as modern watermelons are.

Archaeological discoveries have provided important evidence about the early history of the watermelon. For example, researchers have uncovered seeds from watermelons at an ancient settlement in Libya. [Q34 → seeds]

There is also visual evidence in several Egyptian tombs. Researchers have discovered wall paintings that clearly show this type of fruit. [Q35 → paintings]

Over time, watermelons gradually spread beyond Africa. Historical evidence suggests that around 450 BC they began to appear in Europe. This movement was mainly the result of international trade, which allowed plants and crops to travel between different regions. [Q36 → trade]

In ancient societies, watermelons served several purposes. Of course, they were eaten as food, but once the fruit had been consumed, the thick outer skin was often reused. People sometimes used the empty shell as a container, particularly when traveling long distances. [Q37 → container] Watermelons were also believed to have medicinal value. In Persia, for instance, the fruit was sometimes used to treat certain health problems, including headaches. [Q38 → headaches]

The watermelon continued to change as people began selecting plants with desirable characteristics. Historical evidence suggests that ancient Egyptians, who worked as farmers were among the first to intentionally modify certain features of the fruit. [Q39 → farmers] Much later, plant breeders in the United States continued this process of selection. One of their aims was to produce varieties that were stronger and more resistant to damage, so the fruit would be less likely to become bruised during transport. [Q40 → bruised]
